Are CS Majors Cooked?

A note before we start…

I recognize that as a CS major myself, I’m heavily biased towards one opinion. However, I think that being someone who grew up and did a lot of coding the “old school” way of typing every single line by hand, yet also having the privilege of being young in the age of AI and agentic coding offers me a unique perspective in understanding where the field came from and where it seems to be going by continuously adapting to the uncertain situation CS seems to be in right now.

Additionally, there are just so many opinions out there on AI right now. Everyone has an opinion on it, and none of them are more correct than the other, since no one knows what AI could bring. Whose words do we believe, and whose words do we not? I wrote this post not to convince you of a particular stance on AI and CS, but more so to give you more information, so that you can convince yourself on what you want to believe.

I’m not a CS Major. Why do I care?

I dedicate this essay to non CS majors. This entire post was inspired by one of the high schoolers I was TA’ing for as a part of SHAPE. I had somehow gotten placed into a ChemE class, and one of their constant questions to me throughout the session was, “Will you get a job?” and “What’s your value outside of Claude?”

Although the CS job market is the most talked about effect of AI, I do think that over time (and perhaps within the next few years) AI will not only affect CS, but almost any knowledge and engineering work that exists today. One example of this is mathematics. I, like many, always thought that theory and advanced mathematics were topics that AI could never touch because in the end, it’s just a probabilistic engine with no real reasoning, right? But yet, recently AI has been cracking math that humans have not been able to solve - see ChatGPT solving an Erdos problem and disproving the Jacobian conjecture.

And yet, there are so many less calls saying mathematicians are cooked. All this to say - CS is only the current poster child of AI augmenting/replacing jobs. Even if you’re not as affected right now, the takeaways you get from this essay may be applicable to your job in the future.

The Spectrum of Opinions

To simplify opinions and avoid writing too long of a blog post, I’ve decided to split opinions into some specific subopinions. For our purposes now, SWE = CS major (although I know this isn’t true, for 90% of people this is their intended pathway)

  1. CS Majors and SWE is cooked. There aren’t going to be SWEs in the future
  2. SWE will experience some downturn, but the job will still exist and be needed (but will be significantly augmented by AI)
  3. SWE will not change much jobs-wise (or even grow), but what SWEs do will change a lot

SWE is Cooked.

This was a predominant opinion at the onset of Claude Code, but from what I’ve read and seen, this crowd is dying out a little. That doesn’t mean it’s gone though! See tweet below, making fun of stances 2 & 3):

Source: x.com/alexfmac/status/2047065280912970050

And to be fair, they have a point. According to the IEEE, Computer Science majors face unemployment rates of 6.1%, higher than the average and far higher than majors like Philosophy, which comes in at 3.2%. Hiring into entry level jobs has dropped 73%. And from my own experience, for almost all aspects of simple coding - stuff I used to call “foundational” to learning CS - can be completely done by Sonnet/Opus level AI, not even Fable.

To this set of people, they argue that there is nothing a SWE can do that AI cannot do, saying that everything a SWE can do right now that AI can’t do will just be trained for when the next AI comes out. Since SWE is mostly online and on a computer, the amount of physical work, AI’s greatest weakness right now, that they have to do is minimal. To put an apt comparison, before the existence of calculators there was an occupation called the computer that served to crunch numbers. The job died out because of the invention of calculators and computers that could do their job more accurately and efficiently, and many argue Software Engineers and AI, in a cruel twist of irony, are going in the same direction.

SWE is Shrinking.

I personally think that this opinion is the most likely to be true.

There are some things that AI cannot do - namely, human relationships and community. For example, when AI deletes an entire database for you, someone needs to be delegated the blame of causing the problem and then the responsibility of fixing it. Plus, in regards to finances, with the growing cost of AI, like Uber spending its entire AI budget within 4 months, what model you use and the harnesses that you use will become very important soon, and that skill may be something that is expected of SWEs in the future. Understanding which models to use, especially with the emergence of cheap, open-source Chinese models like the recent Kimi K3 and GLM 5.2 models, will be extremely important.

In addition, I think many people have misconceptions on what CS/SWE actually does. Don’t forget, CS actually stands for something - computer Science. It is still a science - there are real problems to solve that don’t involve being a code monkey. CS involves knowing how to architect systems that protect against malicious actors, using as few resources to be as efficient as possible, etc. While code is how those ideas are implemented, the ideas themselves often still need to be considered by humans - after all, that’s why many vibe coded slop websites often fail under pressure when anything to do with secrets or databases pop up. However, this is still bad news for recent grads. These considerations were traditionally meant for more senior-level SWEs, and for many entry-level jobs, being a code monkey was THE job. Junior SWEs are now being expected to know a lot more system design than they used to, whilst in the past it was mostly about your coding and Leetcode ability. With the latter becoming less important, it’s probably likely that there’ll be less entry-level CS jobs in the future. However, without entry-level engineers, in 20 years there will be no senior-level engineers either. Will AI replace senior SWEs by the time that happens, or will CS start hiring at levels seen back in 2020-21 again?

Speaking of the 2020-21 hiring spree, another reason why the SWE job outlook has experienced significant downturn is because companies overhired during COVID, and are now laying off the massive amounts of employees they hired then. In fact, AI-related layoffs are relatively rare (the one that comes to mind most recently is Meta’s push to become an AI focused company) when in comparison to overhiring-related layoffs. As a result, this may mean that the massive layoffs that we see right now may ease up within a couple of years as companies start adjusting.

SWE is Growing.

The core idea of this is Jevons paradox. Jevons paradox essentially says that when something becomes more efficient to create, the more we use it. While it may not sound like a paradox, the idea is that if something is more efficient, we would need less workers to create it and thus demand for that kind of work would decrease. However, because it’s more efficient, more people will use it, and thus demand for that work will go right back up.

Over the years of history, this paradox has played out multiple times. The invention of the cotton gin was meant to automate cotton picking and reduce slavery, but its invention instead exploded the use of enslaved people in the US. The invention of more efficient coal machines led to the Industrial Revolution that led to an explosion in usage of coal and led to our reliance on it for energy today.

History doesn’t repeat, but it often rhymes, and proponents of this theory say that AI will do the same for SWE. Since AI makes coding easier, more people will use computers and coding. However, they still need someone to understand how to set everything up and make sure the website is functional, and hence, SWEs will still be needed in the future.

So what should you think?

I’ve tried to keep this blog as objective as possible. I don’t believe in telling you what you should think, and I think a little critical thinking goes a long way. That being said, many people do want to tell you what you should think - intentionally or not. Even what I say probably has a hidden agenda because like all humans, I have biases.

A few years ago, Geoffrey Hinton, a godfather of AI, said that radiologists would be replaced within the coming years. So did Jensen Huang. And yet today, demand for radiologists is booming and their salaries have climbed as radiologists do so much more than just looking at scans. The same could be the same for CS, but the point of this example was to tell you that just because someone famous and important like Huang and Hinton say something does not necessarily mean that it is true. Their opinions should be respected, but figure out if what they’re saying is true, and mesh it with your own beliefs instead of blindly taking theirs as your own.

I think a great example of a company doing this is Anthropic. Recently, they’ve been really pushing out their narrative that they are the only people that can be trusted to train AI. Everything from their talk about their so-called J-space and global pause on AI development (and then releasing Fable a few days later) all feels somewhat meant to manipulate the public. While I’m not saying Anthropic is necessarily wrong, companies have an agenda - for you to buy their products.
